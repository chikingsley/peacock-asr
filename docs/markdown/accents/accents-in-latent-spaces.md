# Accents in Latent Spaces

### How AI Hears Accent Strength in English

**By Oscar Friedman and Ilya Usorov**
**May 6, 2025**

---

We work with accents a lot at [BoldVoice](https://boldvoice.com), the AI-powered accent coaching app for non-native English speakers. Accents are subtle patterns in speech — vowel shape, timing, pitch, and more. Usually, you need a linguist to make sense of these qualities. However, our goal at BoldVoice is to get machines to understand accents, and machines don't think like linguists. So, we ask: **how does a machine learning model understand an accent, and specifically, how strong it is?**

---

## The Accent Fingerprint

To begin this journey, we introduce the **"accent fingerprint"** — an embedding generated by inferencing an English speech recording through BoldVoice's large-scale accented speech model.

```
torch.Size([1, 768, 12])
```

*The accent fingerprint embedding dimensions*

In this post we'll show:

- Where the accent fingerprint lives in a latent space
- How distances and directions in that space correspond to accent similarity and language background
- How we use it to coach a non-native English speaker toward the American English accent of an expert accent coach

### The Subjects

- **Victor** — BoldVoice's product management intern; non-native English speaker with a noticeably strong Chinese accent
- **Eliza** — BoldVoice's expert accent coach; native American English speaker; serves as the "target" accent

---

## The Latent Space

To make sense of how the model understands both recordings, 1,000 speech recordings from internal data were populated into a latent space, representing varied levels of accent.

### What the Full Latent Space Contains

The full-dimensional latent space contains information about:

- Speaker identity
- Accent
- Intelligibility
- Emotion
- Other speech characteristics

### Pruning for Accent Strength

For this visualization, the space was pruned to show only information relevant to **"accent strength"** — i.e., how strong is the speaker's accent relative to native speakers of English?

**Method:**

- **PLS (Partial Least Squares) Regression** is applied to identify the latent space directions which correlate most with human accent strength ratings
- **2D UMAP** dimensionality reduction is then applied for visualization
- The **x-axis** = first hidden dimension of accent strength
- The **y-axis** = second hidden dimension of accent strength

### Pseudocode

```python
accent_strength_directions = PLSRegression.fit(train_accent_fingerprints, train_accent_strength_ratings)
accent_strength_features = test_accent_fingerprints[accent_strength_directions]
visualization_features = UMAP(n_components=2).fit_transform(accent_strength_features)
```

> **Footnote 1:** "Latent space" refers to both the full-dimensional space and the pruned 2D visualization for brevity.

> **Footnote 2:** The dimensions are not readily interpretable, are not orthogonal, and are chosen solely to maximize their utility for discriminating between accent strength in L2 English (English as a second language).

## Experiments: Moving Victor Toward Eliza

### Experiment 1 — Denoising (Background Noise Removal)

Victor's original recording was noticeably noisier than Eliza's. The noise was removed to isolate accent differences.

**Result:** The cleaned recording landed very close to the original in the latent space — almost no change in position.

> **Interpretation:** This is a good sanity check — recording quality and background noise level are **not** relevant to accent strength as measured by the model.

---

### Experiment 2 — Accent Conversion

Victor's register (voice pitch) is much lower than Eliza's, potentially making direct mimicry harder. BoldVoice's in-house **accent conversion model** was used to map Eliza's accent onto Victor's voice.

**Result:** The converted recording landed right next to Eliza's original position in the latent space.

> **Note:** Even after conversion, some phonetic differences remain (vowel shapes, emphasis, pitch, timing), but the converted audio gives Victor a much more accessible mimicry target — the target accent is now in his own voice register.

---

### Experiment 3 — Practice (10 minutes)

Victor was given the accent-converted audio and allowed ~10 minutes to practice mimicking it.

**Results after practice:**

- Matched Eliza's **timing, intonation, and stress** quite well
- **Vowel shapes** still not fully accurate
- Position in latent space moved to the **border of Intermediate and Advanced** — a substantial improvement

> **Next step:** For further improvement, BoldVoice's sound-by-sound phonetic analysis would allow Victor to understand the specific patterns in pronunciation and stress contributing to Eliza's accent.

---

## Summary: What Did We Learn?

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| Models can distinguish accent strength | The ML model clearly separates recordings by accent intensity |
| Assessment is language-agnostic | Accent strength measurement appears independent of the speaker's native language |
| Accent strength is malleable | Measurable improvement was achieved in ~10 minutes of practice |
| Voice conversion aids learning | Mapping a target accent onto the learner's own voice provides a more accessible mimicry target |
| Denoising doesn't change accent strength | Acoustic environment changes don't significantly shift latent space position |

## Applications and Next Steps

1. **Learner progress tracking** — Quantitatively measure a learner's accent journey over multiple recordings by tracking distance from a target accent profile in the latent space
2. **ASR evaluation** — Apply the same quantitative approach to rigorously evaluate automatic speech recognition systems for performance variations across different accent strengths
3. **TTS monitoring** — Monitor text-to-speech systems for unwanted changes in accent, often referred to as "accent drift"
